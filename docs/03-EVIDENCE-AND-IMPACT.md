# SANKET — Evidence and Impact

Document 3 of 5 · The real-world record, who is affected, what changes if this exists, and how far it goes

**Scope note.** SANKET is a national watch operated by DHM and NDRRMA, with District Disaster Management Committees holding the approval gate. Alerts reach people by Nepali voice, SMS and WhatsApp — the last carrying the flood map itself. The measurable claim throughout this document is **notification delivered to a named contact with a stated lead time**, which is falsifiable within a single run.

**All figures carry their date. Casualty numbers from the August 2026 event were still moving at the time of writing and should be re-checked before any public use.**

# PART 1 — THE EVIDENCE BASE

## 1.1 The event that defines the problem

### 26 August 2026 — Lhende Khola ice-rock avalanche and cascade flood

| | |
|---|---|
| **Time** | ~08:37 local |
| **Source** | ~600 m wide detachment of glacier ice and rock from ~5,600 m, Langtang Himal, Nepal–Tibet border |
| **Seismic record** | USGS initially catalogued M4.4 earthquake, **reclassified as M5.2 landslide-type event** — the shaking was caused by the collapse |
| **Mechanism** | Collapse → river blockage in the upper Lhende → temporary impoundment → breach → sediment-laden cascade flood |
| **Propagation** | Lhende Khola → Bhotekoshi → Trishuli |
| **Peak stage** | Trishuli reported to rise up to **9 m in 30 minutes** |
| **Downstream reach** | Bodies recovered in Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading, Gorkha, Tanahun, Chitwan, Nawalparasi East and West |
| **Rainfall** | **None reported** at Rasuwa district headquarters |
| **Response mapping** | Copernicus EMS activation **EMSR927** |

**Casualties, as reported, with dates:**

| Date | Source | Figure |
|---|---|---|
| 28 Aug 2026 evening | The Kathmandu Post | 579 deaths |
| 31 Aug 2026 | The Kathmandu Post | 903 deaths |
| ~1 Sep 2026 | NDRRMA bulletin summary via media | 987 bodies recovered in Nepal, 3,916 missing |
| ~1 Sep 2026 | Combined Nepal + Tibet, via media aggregation | past 1,000 dead, past 4,400 missing |

**Infrastructure:** more than ten hydropower projects damaged, including Rasuwagadhi (111 MW), Chilime and Trishuli-3A. Reconstruction cost estimates discussed in Nepali media run into billions of dollars. The Rasuwagadhi–Kerung crossing — Nepal's principal northern trade route to China — was severed.

**Damage assessment, Microsoft AI for Good Lab** (via HDX, quoted by nepal-flood-map.pages.dev): **~37 km² impacted, 4,977 buildings, ~10,200 people.**

**Copernicus EMS EMSR927**, Syapru Besi AOI alone: **more than 240 buildings destroyed, 32 damaged**, from WorldView-3 acquired 27/08/2026 05:05 UTC.

### The consequence that is still live

Two lakes formed. The larger is a **barrier lake** on the Lhende system, impounded behind the debris.

- **28 Aug 2026** — China's Ministry of Water Resources estimated **~1.5–2 million m³**, with a further **~3 million m³** potentially flowing in over three days
- **28 Aug 2026** — Rasuwa officials reported the lake beginning to overflow; **rescue operations halted**
- Chinese authorities deployed an engineering team for aerial survey and 3D modelling

**This is an unresolved hazard with hours-to-days of usable lead time. It is exactly what the system is built to assess.**

## 1.2 The precursor record — the reason this project exists

ICIMOD time-series analysis, reported via Dialogue Earth and PreventionWeb, documents a lake at the **Purepu Glacier, ~35 km upstream of Rasuwagadhi**:

| Date | Observation |
|---|---|
| **July 2023** | Lake formed and drained within about a week; the resulting flood travelled ~10 km downstream |
| **December 2024** | Lake widened again |
| **June 2025** | Lake grew significantly |
| **8 July 2025** | GLOF from Jilong County, Tibet destroyed the Miteri Bridge |

**Three years of satellite-visible change in the corridor that then flooded catastrophically twice.** Every observation came from freely available imagery.

ICIMOD's Sudan Bikash Maharjan has been explicit that impact cannot be predicted from lake size alone — a point reinforced by Thame, where the lake was around 0.05 km² at breach.

## 1.3 The regional pattern

| Date | Event | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **8 Jul 2025** | GLOF, Jilong County (TAR) → Lhende/Bhotekoshi | Miteri Bridge destroyed; ≥9 dead, 19 missing. DHM concluded from Sentinel imagery it was a glacial lake outburst. Minimal rainfall both sides. |
| **14 May 2025** | Tilgau, Humla | Two small glacial lakes burst at night; ice-and-debris avalanche destroyed five wooden bridges, displaced ~32 villagers. **No rainfall reported.** |
| **16 Aug 2024** | Thame, Solukhumbu | Cascading failure: an upper lake (formed late 2000s, expanded to ~0.11 km²) overtopped a moraine-covered bedrock dam, triggering failure of a lower lake that had grown 20% between 1989 and 2024. The breach eroded up to **22 m vertically and 51 m horizontally**. Hyper-concentrated flow carried debris **more than 80 km**. Destroyed 14 properties including a school, health post, five hotels and seven homes. ICIMOD notes five significant GLOFs in the Everest region in under 50 years. |
| **Oct 2023** | South Lhonak, Sikkim | A ~1.66 km² lake outburst, attributed to lateral moraine collapse, killed 55 and destroyed the **1,200 MW Chungthang Dam**. |
| **Jun 2021** | Melamchi | Rain-on-snow plus upstream sediment mobilisation; mechanism contested. |

**ICIMOD figure: more than 25,000 glacial lakes across the Hindu Kush Himalaya.** The ICIMOD/UNDP 2020 assessment identified **47 potentially dangerous glacial lakes** across the Koshi, Gandaki and Karnali basins — **25 in China, 21 in Nepal, 1 in India** — and flagged the Poiqu / Bhote Koshi / Sun Koshi watershed as highly GLOF-prone. That finding predates both recent events in this corridor.

## 1.4 The scientific foundation

| Work | Finding | How it informs SANKET |
|---|---|---|
| **Taylor, Robinson, Dunning, Carr & Westoby (2023)**, *Nat Commun* 14:487 | First global GLOF exposure quantification. ~15 million people exposed globally; **High Mountain Asia most exposed at ~9.3 million**, ~1 million within 10 km of a glacial lake. Danger is driven **not by lake number or growth rate but by population proximity and coping capacity.** | The entire framing of the Exposure Agent — and the justification for why exposure, not lake counting, is the useful output |
| **Shrestha, Steiner et al. (2023)**, *ESSD* 15:3941 | HMAGLOFDB: 697 GLOFs, 1833–2022; 6,906 total fatalities of which 906 attributable to 24 individual events (3× previous estimates); **23% recurring from just three ephemeral ice-dammed lakes**; includes transboundary China→Nepal events | Base rates, the Disaster Memory Agent's schema, and the finding that **recurrence concentrates** — the Purepu/Lhende pattern exactly |
| **NHESS 26:4131 (2026)**, Thame cascading GLOF | Reconstructs a *cascade*: upper lake overtops, triggering lower lake failure. Explicitly names **cascading chains as overlooked in conventional risk assessment.** | The validation case, the multi-lake cascade logic, and the single best citation for why one-lake-at-a-time monitoring fails |
| **Rounce, McKinney, Lala, Byers & Watson (2016)**, *HESS* 20:3455 | Remote hazard and risk assessment framework for glacial lakes in the Nepal Himalaya | The hazard-scoring rubric and downstream-routing approach for exactly this geography |
| **Mergili, Fischer, Krenn & Pudasaini (2017)**, *GMD* 10:553; **r.avaflow v4 (2025)**, *GMD* 18:9879 | Two- and three-phase mass-flow simulation for process chains, including bulk-mass release into a reservoir with dam entrainment | The routing physics, and the credibility answer to "is your simulation real?" |
| **Veh, Korup et al. (2019)**, *Nat Clim Change* 9:379 | **Unchanged frequency of moraine-dammed GLOFs in the Himalaya** | The essential counterweight. We must not claim GLOFs are increasing — the literature genuinely disagrees, and HMAGLOFDB describes the evidence as ambiguous. |
| **ICIMOD/UNDP (2020)** | 47 PDGLs across three countries; documented remote-sensing methodology; cites **Imja Tsho's 2016 lowering plus community EWS** as a working mitigation precedent | The baseline, the methodology citation, and the answer to "what does mitigation actually look like" |
| **Bonafilia et al. (2020)**, *CVPRW* | Sen1Floods11: 4,831 chips, 120,406 km², 11 events | The DL baseline — and the honest statement about how little Himalayan signal it contains |
| **Zhang et al.**, *Landslides* 10.1007/s10346-024-02358-x | South Lhonak GLOF, 55 dead, 1,200 MW dam destroyed | Regional precedent for hydropower cascade loss |

## 1.5 What the experts said should be built

Quoted by Al Jazeera, 28 August 2026:

**Pawan Bhattarai, Tribhuvan University** — the avalanche itself was probably not preventable, but consequences could have been reduced through **better satellite monitoring, rapid detection of river blockage and lake formation, real-time river monitoring, timely evacuation, and stronger Nepal–China transboundary early warning.**

**Rijan Bhakta Kayastha, Kathmandu University** — the ice-avalanche assessment was preliminary, based on discussions with scientists in Nepal and China.

The first quotation is, almost line for line, the specification for this system.

## 1.6 The live attribution dispute

| Party | Position |
|---|---|
| **DHM Flood Forecasting Division** | Icefall and landslide blocked the river ~20 km upstream of the Miteri Bridge, forming a temporary lake that burst. Senior divisional hydrologist Binod Parajuli cited a combination of ice avalanche, permafrost movement and rockslide. |
| **DHM + ICIMOD, subsequently** | Concluded from satellite imagery that it was a **GLOF from a supraglacial lake in Tibet** |
| **geopera.com independent reconstruction** (28 Aug, updated 1 Sep 2026) | **No pre-existing lake drained.** Water came from melted avalanche ice, entrained sediment and the river itself; a sustained dam-and-breach mechanism does not match observed arrival timing at the border. **This analysis publicly retracted its own sediment-volume figure** after finding a parallax error in its stereo geometry. |

**Critical methodological point:** if DHM and ICIMOD both worked from the same Chinese-supplied imagery, they are **one line of evidence, not two.** Agreement between them raises confidence far less than a naive source count suggests. This is the independence check the Verification Agent implements, and this dispute is its live test case.

# PART 2 — IMPACT

## 2.1 Who is exposed

### The corridor

The Lhende → Bhotekoshi → Trishuli corridor runs from ~5,600 m on the Nepal–Tibet border down through:

| Location | District | What is there |
|---|---|---|
| Rasuwagadhi / Timure | Rasuwa | Border crossing, customs, the Miteri Bridge site, Rasuwagadhi hydropower (111 MW) |
| Syapru Besi | Rasuwa | Trailhead settlement, tourism gateway to Langtang, **>240 buildings destroyed per CEMS EMSR927** |
| Chilime, Dhunche | Rasuwa | District headquarters, Chilime hydropower |
| Betrawati, Trishuli Bazaar | Nuwakot | Market towns, Trishuli-3A hydropower |
| Downstream Trishuli | Nuwakot, Dhading, Gorkha, Tanahun, Chitwan, Nawalparasi | Where bodies were recovered — hundreds of km from the source |

### Exposure at the regional scale

- **~9.3 million people** exposed to GLOF hazard across High Mountain Asia, the highest of any region globally (Taylor et al. 2023)
- **~1 million** live within 10 km of a glacial lake
- **>25,000 glacial lakes** across the Hindu Kush Himalaya (ICIMOD)
- **47 potentially dangerous glacial lakes** identified in the Koshi, Gandaki and Karnali basins, **most of them across a border from the people they threaten** (25 China, 21 Nepal, 1 India)

**The key finding to build on:** Taylor et al. established that danger is driven not by lake number or growth rate but by **population proximity and coping capacity**. Coping capacity is the variable a system like this can actually move.

## 2.2 What changes if SANKET exists

### For the district officer

**Today:** the hazard is upstream, across a border, unmonitored. There is no gauge, no feed, no number. The first indication is the river rising.

**With SANKET:** a computed arrival time per settlement for a blockage of stated volume, an inundation extent, a count of buildings and people inside it, which settlements lose all road egress, and mapped routes to high ground. Not a prediction — a scenario, labelled as one, with its parameters attached. It is the tabletop exercise DRR officers already run on paper, made spatial and instant.

**The measurable change: lead time moves from *unknown* to *computed*.**

### For DHM

**Today:** every operational early-warning channel is anchored to rainfall. Gauges are downstream of the hazard.

**With SANKET:** a non-meteorological hazard channel. Blockage detection from radar imagery within days of formation — including under monsoon cloud, when optical is blind. And a quantified statement of *where to spend monitoring effort*, from the sensitivity analysis: if breach duration dominates the answer and population source barely moves it, that is a research priority, not just a chart.

### For NDRRMA and BIPAD

**Today:** BIPAD records incidents, damage and loss, down to municipality and ward. It records what has happened.

**With SANKET:** a forward-looking exposure layer that pairs with that record. The two are complementary — the historical record grounds base rates, and the scenario layer projects forward.

### For ICIMOD and researchers

**Today:** the Purepu analysis existed. It was published as analysis, in a report, read by specialists.

**With SANKET:** the same science, running continuously, queryable by someone with no remote-sensing training. The gap SANKET closes is not scientific — it is operational.

### For the wider Nepali response community

**Today:** as the Nepal Flood Response Map team put it — every project rebuilds its own database and creates competing versions of the same picture. Their own honest note: *"It demonstrates the unified-data pattern; it is not yet a shared national system."*

**With SANKET:** the lakehouse is published back to HDX and HuggingFace, licence-per-layer, with every layer carrying its source, dates, confidence tier, **what it cannot tell you**, and which other layers share its origin. The next team builds on it instead of rebuilding it.

## 2.3 What we can honestly claim as impact

**Defensible now:**

| Claim | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Lead time becomes computable per settlement for a blockage of stated volume | The scenario grid, with parameters attached and uncertainty bands |
| Exposure estimates can be validated against an official product | Confusion matrix, IoU, precision and recall against Copernicus EMS EMSR927 |
| A three-year precursor signal was detectable in free imagery before two fatal floods | The lake-area time series, reproduced under a date lock with a rejection count |
| Rainfall does not explain either event | Basin precipitation percentile against a 20-year climatology |
| A reusable open dataset exists where none did | The published lakehouse with its licence table |
| Contested evidence can be handled without manufacturing a conclusion | The verification table's *insufficient — no claim issued* row |

**Not claimable, and we should say so before anyone asks:**

- We cannot claim we would have saved lives on 26 August. The collapse was not forecastable.
- We cannot claim a reduction in casualties, because we have not been deployed and there is no counterfactual.
- We cannot claim accuracy figures that mean anything from a validation set of four events.
- We cannot claim the system replaces DHM, ICIMOD or NDRRMA. It is a layer on top of work they already do.

**The honest framing:** this converts *unknowable* into *estimable with stated uncertainty*. That is a real change, and it is a smaller claim than most projects in this space make.

## 2.4 Impact pathways

**Immediate — the live barrier lake.** A scenario assessment for an impoundment that exists right now, with hours-to-days of lead time, in a corridor with no upstream monitoring. This is the only pathway that could matter this month.

**Short term — preparedness planning.** Arrival times and evacuation routes for the settlements below. Siren placement. Safe assembly point designation. Which of these is a tabletop exercise input rather than a live alert, and we should say so.

**Medium term — monitoring prioritisation.** The sensitivity analysis says which unknown moves the answer most. That is a directly actionable recommendation about where to place instruments — and instruments are what Nepal actually lacks in this catchment.

**Medium term — the shared data foundation.** Published back out, reducing duplicated effort across every Nepali response tool built after this one.

**Long term — transboundary early warning.** SANKET does not solve the diplomatic problem. But a working, open, evidence-traceable system that demonstrates what shared imagery makes possible is a more concrete argument for a data-sharing arrangement than a policy paper is.

## 2.5 Scaling

**Same corridor, deeper.** Add the Trishuli's full downstream reach to Chitwan. Add ward-level resolution. Add more scenarios.

**Same hazard, more corridors.** The pipeline is configuration, not code — the AOI is a bounding box in a config file. The 47 identified PDGLs across the Koshi, Gandaki and Karnali basins are the natural expansion set. Tilgau in Humla (May 2025) and Thame in Solukhumbu (August 2024) are ready-made second and third deployments with published ground truth.

**Same architecture, other hazards.** Landslide dams follow an identical blockage-to-breach logic. Debris flows share the routing engine. Neither is in scope now, and both should be a roadmap slide rather than a claim.

**Regional.** High Mountain Asia holds ~9.3 million exposed people across Nepal, India, Pakistan, China, Bhutan and Central Asia. Everything SANKET depends on — OPERA, Sentinel, Copernicus DEM, HMAGLOFDB, OSM — is global. Nothing in the architecture is Nepal-specific except the datasets loaded into it.

**Cost.** The demonstration runs on free satellite data, open-weight models on free API tiers, and one laptop. Marginal cost per additional corridor is data storage and a person's time — not licences. **That is the property that makes national coverage arguable rather than aspirational.**

## 2.6 Sustainability and ownership

Built on Nepali government data, free and openly licensed Earth observation, and open-source models. Runs offline. No dependency on a foreign commercial API.

That last point is not a technical preference. **A national early-warning capability that depends on a foreign commercial service is not a national capability.** The natural institutional homes are DHM, NDRRMA and ICIMOD — all three already produce the inputs.

The published lakehouse survives the project regardless of what happens to the application, which is the appropriate outcome for something built almost entirely from other people's open data.

## 2.7 The honest limits on impact

**We cannot see all the dangerous lakes.** 10 m imagery cannot reliably detect water bodies below ~0.003 km². Thame's lake was ~0.05 km². Some of the ones that matter next will be smaller.

**Monsoon is when we see least and need most.** Optical is largely unusable. Radar mitigates but layover and shadow in steep valleys are real, and a lake can form and drain entirely between passes — apparently what happened at Purepu in July 2023, within about a week.

**Post-event terrain is wrong.** The channel has been reshaped by an enormous volume of debris. Every routing result computed on pre-event terrain is wrong in ways we cannot correct without new survey.

**Population data is modelled, not counted.** It estimates usual residence and cannot show displacement after 26 August. Counts are order-of-magnitude, not operational.

**The source is in China.** No ground data, no gauges, no guaranteed imagery sharing. In August 2026 the critical imagery reached DHM *through Chinese authorities*, not through any automated channel. **No amount of code fixes this.**

**False alarms have real costs.** Evacuating a valley is disruptive and erodes trust in future warnings. Every alert requires DHM or NDRRMA confirmation before dissemination, and every evacuation decision belongs to local authority. This is decision *support*.

**The right precedent is ICIMOD's own:** continuous monitoring, safe lowering of lakes where feasible, early warning, and timely evacuation — with Imja Tsho's 2016 lowering plus a community-based early warning system as the working example of what mitigation looks like when it succeeds.

# PART 3 — POSITIONING

## 3.1 Against what exists

| System | What it does | What it cannot do |
|---|---|---|
| **BIPAD (NDRRMA)** | Nepal's national disaster information system — incidents, damage and loss, hazard and vulnerability, real-time, per-municipality subdomains, VisRisk visualisation | Records what already happened. No forward simulation, no satellite analysis, no reasoning. |
| **DHM river watch / flood monitoring** | Real-time gauges, flood forecasting, 24/7 monsoon service, toll-free 1155 | Gauges sit **downstream of the hazard** and are rainfall-anchored. Structurally blind to a dry-day ice collapse in Tibet. |
| **ICIMOD** | The authoritative science: lake inventories, PDGL identification, cryosphere monitoring, post-event assessment | Expert-mediated, at report cadence. The Purepu time series existed as analysis, not as a running system a district officer can query. |
| **Google Flood Hub** | Excellent global riverine forecasting, free, CC BY 4.0 | Rainfall–runoff driven. Cryospheric cascade is outside model scope. Access waitlisted. |
| **Copernicus EMS** | Rapid mapping on activation (EMSR927 for this event) | **Post-event.** Answers what was destroyed, not what is about to be. |
| **Nepal Flood Response Map** | Open-data triage for this exact corridor, honest published methodology, **deliberately no AI** | A static post-event lakehouse snapshot and a fixed arithmetic score. No simulation, no reasoning, no contradiction handling. |
| **Geospatial LLM agents** (GeoGPT, LLM-Geo, GIS Copilot, GeoLLM-Squad, Geo-OLM) | Credible research on tool-calling over geospatial APIs | Task-agnostic. No hazard physics, no domain evidence model, no verification layer, no geography. |

**Note:** at least three teams are working this corridor with the same open data. Differentiation is the reasoning layer and the simulation, not the map.

## 3.2 The four genuinely novel things

**1. Evidence adjudication with veto power.** Not a confidence percentage appended to an answer — a separate stage with an independence check, a claim-licensing check, and authority to emit *insufficient evidence, no claim issued*. We found no operational climate-risk system that does this. The Rasuwa attribution dispute is a live, unplanned test case.

**2. A temporal firewall as a first-class feature.** Retrospective hazard demonstrations are almost all quietly hindsight-contaminated. Making the cutoff a required argument on every retrieval — SQL, satellite, *and* documents — with a visible count of refused records, turns "we could have caught this" from a claim into something checkable.

**3. Blockage-to-breach as the monitoring unit.** Existing GLOF work monitors lakes. Both events that killed people here involved a **river blockage forming and failing** — transient, radar-detectable within days, terrain-quantifiable, with genuinely usable lead time. That is the right object, and it is what sits on the Lhende right now.

**4. Deliberately sovereign and offline-capable.** Free data, open weights, local fallback, one laptop. An argument, not a constraint.

## 3.3 The line

> Their map answers *who needs help now*, from a static post-event snapshot, and they were right to keep AI out of it.
>
> We answer a different question: *what is about to happen, and who is in the way.* The barrier lake is still there.
>
> So we built the shared foundation they said didn't exist yet — every layer carrying its source, its date, its licence, and an explicit statement of what it **cannot** tell you — and on top of it, a system that investigates rather than summarises.
>
> The agent that matters most is the one that refuses.
>
> Nepal already had the data. SANKET is the system that reads it.
